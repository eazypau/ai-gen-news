Malaysia's Social Media Ban for Under-16s Now Enforced — Platforms Must Verify Ages Using MyKad

Malaysia began enforcing its landmark social media ban for children under 16 on June 1, 2026, under the Online Safety Act 2025 (ONSA) — joining Australia and a growing list of countries that have moved to restrict minors' access to social platforms amid mounting evidence of harms related to screen addiction, cyberbullying, and exposure to inappropriate content. The enforcement phase marks a significant transition from the regulatory frameworks announced in 2025 to active compliance obligations for platforms operating in Malaysia.

Social media platforms with at least 8 million users in Malaysia — including Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, X (formerly Twitter), and YouTube — are required under the Child Protection Code (CPC) and Risk Mitigation Code (RMC) to implement robust age-verification systems. Users must verify their age using government-issued identity documents recognised by Malaysian authorities, primarily the MyKad (national identity card) for Malaysian citizens or a valid passport for foreign residents. Platforms that fail to implement compliant systems face fines of up to RM10 million under ONSA.

Malaysia's Communications and Multimedia Commission (MCMC) confirmed that age verification for existing users will be rolled out progressively over a six-month window, meaning full enforcement will be completed by December 2026. During the transition period, platforms are required to identify and flag potentially underage users, providing them a one-month window to download or transfer their data before account restrictions are applied.

The policy has generated significant public debate. Supporters, including child safety advocates and parents' groups, argue that unrestricted access to social media is demonstrably harmful for children's mental health and development, citing studies linking heavy adolescent social media use to depression, anxiety, and disrupted sleep. Critics — including digital rights organisations like Centre for Independent Journalism — raised concerns about privacy implications of mandatory identity verification and the potential for government surveillance of citizens' online activities.

Communications Minister Teo Nie Ching said the government is committed to protecting children online while respecting adult users' privacy rights, and that the verification system is designed with privacy safeguards that prevent platforms from retaining copies of identity documents. She urged parents to work alongside the regulatory framework by discussing responsible digital habits with their children rather than relying solely on platform restrictions. The MCMC has established a dedicated hotline for complaints about platforms failing to comply with age-verification requirements.
