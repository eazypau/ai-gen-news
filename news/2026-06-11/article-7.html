Monthly Fuel Subsidy Spending Climbs to RM7.5 Billion in April as Oil Prices Stay Elevated

Malaysia's monthly fuel subsidy expenditure surged to RM7.5 billion in April 2026, highlighting the growing fiscal strain from global oil price volatility and the government's ongoing commitment to protecting lower-income households from sharp increases at the pump. The figure represents a dramatic increase from the approximately RM700 million per month recorded before recent oil price escalations, underscoring how sensitive Malaysia's subsidy bill is to movements in global crude prices.

The Ministry of Finance confirmed the spending figure while briefing parliamentary committees on fiscal outlook, noting that while the BUDI95 targeted subsidy programme has narrowed eligibility compared to the previous blanket subsidy scheme, the sheer volume of eligible vehicles and the elevated global oil price environment have pushed costs far above initial projections. The government had initially budgeted RM49 billion for total subsidy spending in 2026, a 14.1% reduction from 2025 levels.

The April figure has reignited pressure on the government to accelerate the rationalisation of RON95 petrol subsidies for higher-income earners. Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim has said the government has agreed in principle to remove the subsidy for the T20 income group, but a final decision on the exact threshold — whether to cut off the top 5%, 10%, 15%, or 20% of earners — has not yet been announced, with the decision reportedly sensitive given the approaching election season.

Non-subsidised RON95 is currently priced at RM4.02 per litre, meaning eligible Malaysians who pay the capped price of RM1.99 are effectively receiving a subsidy of over RM2 per litre. For a driver filling a typical 50-litre tank, this translates to over RM100 in direct savings per fill-up — a significant household benefit that the government is wary of removing abruptly, particularly as the cost of living remains a top public concern.

Economists have warned that the ballooning subsidy bill could derail Malaysia's fiscal consolidation targets if oil prices remain elevated through the second half of 2026. The government is also facing pressure from the International Monetary Fund and credit rating agencies to demonstrate a credible path toward deficit reduction, with the fuel subsidy burden cited as one of the key risks to Malaysia's medium-term fiscal outlook.
