US-China Tech Rivalry Seen as Boon for Malaysia's Semiconductor Sector

The intensifying technology rivalry between the United States and China is creating significant new opportunities for Malaysia to attract supply chain investments, particularly in the semiconductor, electrical and electronics (E&E), data centre, and electric vehicle (EV) sectors, economists and industry analysts told the New Straits Times. As American and Chinese firms accelerate their decoupling strategies, Malaysia has emerged as a preferred alternative manufacturing and technology hub in Southeast Asia, offering skilled labour, established infrastructure, and favourable regulatory conditions.

Analysts from Hong Leong Investment Bank (HLIB) highlighted that the China+1 supply chain strategy — where multinationals seek to diversify manufacturing away from sole reliance on China — has been one of the most consequential trends shaping Malaysia's industrial landscape in recent years. Malaysia's semiconductor ecosystem, centred in Penang but expanding to Kulim and Johor, is benefiting from orders redirected from Chinese suppliers and the establishment of new facilities by American, Taiwanese, and European chip firms seeking geopolitically neutral production bases.

The data centre sector is closely linked to this trend, with demand for AI-ready computing infrastructure driving hyperscale investments that require advanced chip packaging and testing capabilities available in Malaysia. Major cloud providers including Microsoft, Amazon Web Services, and Google have made multi-billion dollar commitments to Malaysian data centre campuses, creating an interconnected ecosystem of semiconductor manufacturing, advanced packaging, and AI infrastructure that analysts believe has strong long-term growth prospects.

However, economists cautioned that realising these opportunities fully requires Malaysia to navigate carefully. The country must maintain its policy of engaging constructively with both the United States and China — its two largest trading partners — without being seen as tilting decisively toward either side. Any perception of alignment could invite retaliation or exclusion from trade and investment flows. Malaysia's consistent position of ASEAN neutrality and its emphasis on economic pragmatism have thus far served it well in this balancing act.

Additionally, experts noted that Malaysia must urgently address skills gaps in advanced semiconductor design and AI engineering to capture higher-value segments of the supply chain. While Malaysia excels at back-end chip packaging and testing, competing in front-end wafer fabrication and chip design — where the greatest margins lie — will require sustained investment in technical education, research and development, and talent attraction programmes targeting the Malaysian diaspora abroad.
