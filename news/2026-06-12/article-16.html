Malaysia's Flood-Related Losses Drop to RM636 Million in 2025, Terengganu Worst Hit

Malaysia's total economic losses from flooding declined significantly in 2025, falling to RM636.9 million from RM933.4 million in 2024 — a reduction of nearly 32%, according to data compiled by the National Disaster Management Agency (NADMA) and state emergency management authorities. While the improvement is welcomed, officials caution that the absolute figure remains substantial and underscores the need for continued investment in flood resilience infrastructure, early warning systems, and climate adaptation measures.

Terengganu recorded the highest flood-related losses among all Malaysian states in 2025, at RM71.8 million, reflecting the state's persistent vulnerability to monsoon flooding along its coastal plains and river basins. Kelantan came second with RM67.1 million in losses, followed by Johor at RM29.8 million. The East Coast states — Terengganu, Kelantan, and Pahang — historically bear the brunt of Malaysia's annual northeast monsoon floods, which typically occur between November and March, while Johor faces year-round flooding risks due to its complex river systems.

The government has attributed the improved figures partly to investments in flood mitigation infrastructure completed in recent years, including new retention ponds, upgraded river widening projects, and improved drainage systems in urban areas. NADMA also credited enhanced community preparedness programmes and the expansion of the SMART tunnel network in Kuala Lumpur with preventing more significant losses during the past monsoon season. However, officials acknowledged that climate change is making rainfall patterns less predictable, with more intense precipitation events occurring outside traditional monsoon periods.

Looking ahead to the 2025/2026 northeast monsoon season — which begins in November — meteorologists from the Malaysian Meteorological Department (MetMalaysia) have flagged the potential transition to El Niño conditions in June-July 2026, which could bring prolonged dry spells to parts of the peninsula followed by more intense rainfall events. Authorities are urging state governments to complete flood infrastructure works before the monsoon season and ensure evacuation centres are fully stocked and operational.

Environmental groups have called for the government to address the root causes of Malaysia's flooding vulnerability, including deforestation on hillslopes that reduces water retention capacity, poorly planned urban development that encroaches on flood plains, and inadequate stormwater management in rapidly developing peri-urban areas. They argue that flood losses, while declining, represent only a fraction of the total economic and human cost of Malaysia's chronic flooding problem, which is expected to worsen as global temperatures rise.
