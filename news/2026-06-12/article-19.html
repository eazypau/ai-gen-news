World Bank: Malaysia's BUDI95 Fuel Subsidy Reform is a Step in the Right Direction

The World Bank has endorsed Malaysia's BUDI95 targeted fuel subsidy programme as "a step in the right direction," highlighting it as one of the more thoughtfully designed subsidy reform initiatives in the developing world. In a policy assessment published earlier this year, the Washington-based multilateral institution praised Malaysia's transition from a blanket RON95 fuel subsidy — which benefited all vehicle owners equally regardless of income — to a targeted mechanism that ensures subsidised prices are directed primarily toward lower and middle-income Malaysians who genuinely need the support.

The World Bank analysis noted that under the previous blanket subsidy system, a disproportionate share of government spending benefited high-income households that owned multiple vehicles and drove more kilometres annually. By introducing the BUDI95 mechanism — which uses MyKad identity verification at petrol pumps and links subsidised allocations to JPJ driving licences — Malaysia has created a more equitable distribution system that significantly reduces "leakage" to foreign nationals and commercial operators who were previously able to access Malaysian subsidised fuel.

From a fiscal sustainability perspective, the World Bank highlighted that BUDI95 is saving the Malaysian government billions of ringgit annually compared to the uncapped blanket subsidy, freeing fiscal space for productive investments in infrastructure, education, and healthcare. This is particularly significant in the context of the West Asia crisis, which has driven global oil prices higher and increased the cost of maintaining fuel subsidies. The targeted approach ensures that the government's fiscal burden scales roughly with the number of eligible citizens rather than the global price of oil.

The report also noted that BUDI95 is being watched closely by other developing nations in Southeast Asia, South Asia, and Africa that are grappling with similar challenges of reforming costly and inefficient universal fuel subsidies without triggering political backlash. Malaysia's success in implementing the transition with relatively limited public protest — partly attributed to the government's communication strategy and the preservation of the subsidised price point at RM1.99/litre — is seen as instructive for other reform-minded governments.

Remaining challenges identified by the World Bank include ensuring that rural communities with limited public transport access and lower-income urban commuters who rely heavily on personal vehicles for work are adequately protected as the monthly subsidy quota is potentially further reduced. The bank recommended that Malaysia consider supplementing the fuel subsidy rationalisation with enhanced cash transfer programmes or public transport subsidies to ensure that no vulnerable group is left behind in the transition to a more targeted subsidy regime.
