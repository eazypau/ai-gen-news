Malaysia Evaluates Alternative Missile Suppliers After Norway Revokes Naval Licence

Malaysia is actively evaluating alternative missile suppliers following Norway's decision to revoke the export licence for the Naval Strike Missile (NSM) system, which had been earmarked for the Royal Malaysian Navy (RMN). The revocation, attributed to evolving Norwegian export control policies amid heightened global security concerns, has left Malaysia needing to identify a replacement anti-ship missile system capable of meeting the RMN's operational requirements within the timeframe of its naval modernisation programme.

The Naval Strike Missile is a stealth anti-ship and land-attack missile developed by Norway's Kongsberg Defence & Aerospace, and has been selected by numerous NATO navies as well as several Asian partners. Malaysia had been in procurement discussions for the NSM as part of the upgrade of its Littoral Combat Ship (LCS) programme, which has already faced significant delays and cost overruns. The licence revocation adds a further complication to a programme that is already under intense scrutiny from the Malaysian Parliament and the public.

Defence Minister Khaled Nordin confirmed that the Ministry of Defence (MINDEF) has initiated an assessment of alternative missile systems from various international suppliers, including options from France, MBDA's Exocet family, India's BrahMos programme, and American and Turkish alternatives. The evaluation will consider not only technical performance specifications but also the suppliers' ability to provide technology transfer, local industrial participation, and long-term maintenance support — priorities that Malaysia has emphasised in recent defence procurement decisions.

The episode has prompted broader discussion among defence analysts about Malaysia's approach to arms procurement and the risks of relying on single-source suppliers for critical weapon systems. Analysts recommend that MINDEF diversify its defence industrial partnerships to reduce exposure to export control volatility, which has become an increasingly common tool used by Western governments to manage the end-use of advanced military technologies. Malaysia's longstanding policy of non-alignment means it has traditionally sought to maintain procurement relationships with both Western and non-Western suppliers.

The RMN's modernisation programme, centred on the LCS fleet, is designed to give Malaysia a credible naval deterrent capability in the South China Sea, where overlapping territorial claims with China and other nations remain a persistent source of regional tension. Defence observers emphasise that delays in equipping the LCS fleet with effective missile systems could leave a capability gap during a period of heightened maritime security challenges in Malaysian waters. The government has pledged to resolve the procurement issue expeditiously without compromising on the quality of the final system selected.
