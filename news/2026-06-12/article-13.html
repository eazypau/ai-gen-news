Ringgit Set to Reach RM3.95 Against USD — Asia's Top Currency of 2025

The Malaysian ringgit, which surged more than 10% against the US dollar in 2025 to earn the title of Asia's best-performing currency, is widely expected to continue its appreciation trajectory in 2026, with analysts projecting a further strengthening to as high as RM3.95 per dollar by year-end. The ringgit has been supported by a combination of improving fiscal fundamentals, strong foreign direct investment inflows, robust export performance led by electronics and palm oil, and a narrowing interest rate differential with the United States.

Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM), the country's central bank, has maintained a steady monetary policy stance in 2026, keeping the Overnight Policy Rate (OPR) at 3.00% while managing liquidity conditions to support economic growth without stoking inflation. The central bank has also been active in the foreign exchange market, drawing down its international reserves — which exceed USD 100 billion — to smooth excessive ringgit volatility. BNM Governor Dato' Abdul Rasheed Ghaffour has emphasised that BNM is not targeting any specific ringgit level but is focused on ensuring orderly market conditions.

Malaysia's economic fundamentals are seen as fundamentally supportive of a stronger ringgit. The current account has remained in surplus, reflecting Malaysia's status as a net exporter of energy and electronics. Foreign portfolio investment inflows into Bursa Malaysia and Malaysian Government Securities (MGS) have accelerated as global investors seek higher returns in emerging Asian markets. The FBM KLCI's positive performance year-to-date has attracted regional fund flows that have also supported the currency.

The ringgit's appreciation is having mixed effects on different sectors of the Malaysian economy. Exporters, particularly those in the palm oil, rubber, and electronics industries who invoice in US dollars, face margin compression as their ringgit-denominated revenues decline. However, importers and manufacturers who rely on imported inputs benefit from reduced input costs. Consumers also see some relief from the strengthening ringgit through lower prices for imported consumer goods and services, which helps ease inflationary pressures.

Currency strategists at major investment banks broadly agree that the ringgit's medium-term outlook remains positive, underpinned by Malaysia's improving fiscal position as subsidy rationalisation measures reduce the government's spending burden and diversified FDI inflows provide a structural boost to the balance of payments. Risks to the outlook include a potential global recession triggered by escalating trade tensions, a sharp reversal in commodity prices, or a flight-to-safety episode that strengthens the US dollar across emerging market currencies.
