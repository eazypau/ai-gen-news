Malaysia's Semiconductor Talent Gap: A Tenfold Shortage Threatening Chip Ambitions

Technology TechWire Asia / SEMICON SEA 2026 8 June 2026

Malaysia's bid to become a global semiconductor powerhouse is facing a formidable internal constraint: a staggering shortage of qualified engineers. The industry requires an estimated 50,000 skilled engineers to meet current demand, yet Malaysian universities produce only around 5,000 engineering graduates per year — a tenfold gap that has become one of the most pressing challenges spotlighted at SEMICON SEA 2026.

In Penang alone, the epicentre of Malaysia's chip manufacturing ecosystem, the industry is seeking between 12,000 and 15,000 additional technical positions by the end of 2026. Only 15 to 20 percent of engineering graduates possess the microelectronics, materials science, or automation expertise the sector requires, meaning the effective annual supply of job-ready talent is far below even the most conservative estimates of demand.

The problem is compounded by chronic brain drain. Malaysia loses an average of 15 percent of its skilled technical workforce each year to higher-paying markets in Singapore, Taiwan, the United States and Europe. Vacancy rates for advanced packaging and verification engineering roles in Penang exceeded 30 percent through mid-2024 and have shown little improvement since.

The mismatch between university curricula and industry needs is a structural issue that experts say has persisted for years. Many programmes in Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) institutions remain misaligned with the fast-evolving demands of chip design, fabrication and testing. Industry groups have called on the government to overhaul education content and fast-track specialist training programmes.

The government's National Semiconductor Strategy (NSS) targets developing 60,000 highly skilled engineers by 2030, backed by a pledge of MYR25 billion (approximately USD5.7 billion) over the next decade to attract foreign investment and build domestic talent pipelines. Whether those targets can be met on time — given the current pace of graduate production and ongoing brain drain — remains an open question for policymakers and investors watching Malaysia's semiconductor story unfold.
