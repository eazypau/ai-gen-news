Autism Families in Malaysia Face Costly Therapy and Years-Long Diagnosis Waits

Malaysiakini / Free Malaysia Today | 27 June 2026

A special report on Malaysia's autism care landscape has highlighted the severe challenges facing families of children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), including prohibitive private therapy costs, public system waiting lists stretching up to several years for diagnosis and early intervention services, and deep anxiety about the long-term futures of their children. Advocates and affected families are calling on the government to substantially increase funding for specialised support services and streamline pathways for early diagnosis and intervention.

The cost of applied behaviour analysis (ABA) therapy, speech therapy, and occupational therapy — considered the gold standard interventions for children with ASD — can amount to RM2,000–RM5,000 per month or more at private centres, a sum that is out of reach for most Malaysian families without substantial disposable income. Public hospital-based services are heavily subsidised but overwhelmed by demand, with waiting lists for government developmental paediatric assessments reported at 18 months to three years in major urban hospitals.

Early diagnosis and intervention are critical in ASD, with research consistently showing that children who receive structured therapy before the age of five achieve significantly better long-term outcomes in communication, social skills, and independence. The current delays in the Malaysian public system mean that many children miss this critical window, resulting in greater support needs and higher lifetime costs to families and the state.

The National Autism Society of Malaysia (NASOM) and other advocacy groups have renewed calls for the government to recognise ASD as a national health priority, include ASD-specific therapy in the scope of the national health insurance scheme (MySalam/Peka B40), and significantly expand the number of trained therapists working in government facilities. Several pilot programmes offering community-based early intervention at health clinics (klinik kesihatan) have shown promising results and advocates are pushing for a national scale-up.

Parents interviewed for the report expressed fear about what will happen to their children once they age out of school-based support and enter adulthood, noting that Malaysia lacks adequate supported employment, independent living, and residential care services for autistic adults. They urged the government to take a whole-of-life approach to autism support rather than focusing solely on early childhood interventions, and called for the disability welfare system to be adequately resourced and stigma-free.
