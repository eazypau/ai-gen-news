PAS-Bersatu Rift: Decision on PN Coalition Future Looms

Malaysiakini · 23 June 2026 · Politics

The future of the Perikatan Nasional (PN) coalition hangs in the balance as PAS and Bersatu — the two largest component parties — continue to navigate a bitter falling-out that shows no signs of imminent resolution. PAS formally broke off political cooperation with Bersatu on June 8, a dramatic move that shattered the working relationship between the two parties that had been the backbone of the opposition coalition since its formation. Despite the severance of direct cooperation, both parties have technically retained their membership of PN, leaving the coalition's structure in a state of profound ambiguity.

Former Prime Minister Tan Sri Muhyiddin Yassin attended a key PN supreme council meeting this week at which the question of Bersatu's continued membership in the coalition was formally placed on the agenda. Sources familiar with the proceedings indicated that PAS leaders pushed for a resolution that would clearly define the terms under which Bersatu could remain within PN, or alternatively, formalise the party's exit from the coalition. The outcome of the meeting is expected to set the direction for PN's approach to the upcoming Johor state election and future contests.

Among the most contentious issues being discussed is the situation in Kedah, where PAS leads the state government. Reports have circulated that PAS is considering removing Bersatu representatives from the Kedah state executive council, a move that would represent an unprecedented rupture in their governing partnership. Such a step would effectively end the model of PAS-Bersatu power sharing that has underpinned PN governance in the states where the coalition holds power, and would signal that the split between the two parties is irreconcilable in the short term.

The roots of the PAS-Bersatu rift lie in months of escalating disagreements over candidate seat allocations for the multiple state elections being held in 2026, as well as broader strategic differences over how PN should position itself in the evolving Malaysian political landscape. PAS, emboldened by its strong showing in GE15, has sought a larger share of contested seats and greater influence over coalition policy, while Bersatu under Muhyiddin has resisted what it characterises as PAS overreach. Personal tensions between party leaders on both sides have reportedly compounded the structural disagreements.

Political analysts are divided over whether the rift will lead to a full split or whether pragmatic considerations will ultimately push both parties back towards some form of cooperation, particularly as the next general election approaches. A fractured PN would significantly benefit the ruling PH-BN unity government by reducing the effectiveness of the opposition and potentially handing seats to the government in constituencies where PN previously unified the Malay-Muslim vote. For now, both PAS and Bersatu appear to be hardening their positions, and the coming weeks of state elections are likely to test whether the coalition can function even in a reduced or informal capacity.
