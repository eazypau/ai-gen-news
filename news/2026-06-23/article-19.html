TMJ: Malaysia Must Fix Domestic Football Before Eyeing World Cup Hosting

New Straits Times · 23 June 2026 · Sports

Tunku Ismail Sultan Ibrahim, the Crown Prince of Johor and one of Malaysian football's most prominent and outspoken figures, has delivered a blunt assessment of the country's prospects as a future FIFA World Cup host, declaring that Malaysia should put aside any such ambitions until it has addressed the deep-seated problems afflicting its own domestic game. Speaking as World Cup fever grips the nation amid the ongoing 2026 tournament, TMJ — as he is popularly known — said that poor stadium infrastructure and the ongoing crisis of unpaid player salaries in the M-League represent fundamental failings that must be resolved before Malaysia can credibly position itself on the global football stage as a prospective host.

The Crown Prince's remarks carry particular weight given his direct involvement in Malaysian football. As a long-time patron of Johor Darul Ta'zim (JDT), the club he transformed from a mid-table outfit into Southeast Asia's most dominant and professionally run football franchise, TMJ has a track record of demonstrating what is achievable when football is managed with proper governance, investment, and accountability. His willingness to contrast JDT's standards with the structural shortcomings of Malaysian football more broadly has made him a divisive but widely respected voice on the sport's governance challenges in the country.

The issue of unpaid salaries in the M-League has been a recurring scandal that has dogged Malaysian club football for years. Multiple clubs across the top and lower tiers of the domestic pyramid have repeatedly failed to pay their players on time — or at all — leaving footballers, including foreign imports who relocate their families to Malaysia, in financially precarious situations. The Football Association of Malaysia (FAM) and the Malaysia Football League (MFL) have introduced various compliance mechanisms over the years, but enforcement has been inconsistent and the underlying financial fragility of many clubs remains unaddressed. For TMJ, this represents not just a welfare issue but a reputational problem that undermines Malaysia's image as a serious football country.

Stadium infrastructure is the other major obstacle TMJ identified. Hosting a FIFA World Cup requires a network of venues that meet the most exacting international standards for capacity, safety, media facilities, and spectator experience. Malaysia's stadium stock, with some notable exceptions such as the Bukit Jalil National Stadium, is ageing and in need of substantial renovation or replacement. The capital expenditure required to bring multiple venues to FIFA's minimum hosting standards would run into the tens of billions of ringgit — funds that TMJ and others argue would be far better spent on developing grassroots football, coaching infrastructure, and the professional league itself before any hosting bid is seriously contemplated.

TMJ's comments have landed in the middle of a national conversation energised by World Cup fever. With Malaysians staying up through the night to watch the 2026 tournament unfold across the Atlantic, casual discussions about what it might be like for Malaysia to one day host a World Cup have become commonplace on social media and in coffee shops. While the romanticism of such a vision is understandable, TMJ's challenge to the football community serves as a necessary corrective: genuine football ambition must be grounded in the unglamorous but essential work of building a sustainable, accountable, and well-funded domestic game.
