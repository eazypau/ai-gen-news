Malaysia's Data Centre Market to Soar from US$4bn to US$13.6bn by 2030

KPMG Malaysia · 23 June 2026 · Technology

Malaysia's data centre market is on course for explosive growth, with a new analysis by KPMG Malaysia projecting that the sector will expand at a compound annual growth rate of 22.4% to reach US$13.6 billion by 2030, up from approximately US$4 billion today. The forecast underscores the country's emergence as Southeast Asia's dominant data centre hub, a position built on a combination of government incentives, strategic geography, political stability, and access to competitive energy resources. The scale of projected growth reflects not just an incremental uptick in demand but a structural shift in how global technology firms are building their cloud and AI infrastructure across the region.

The numbers behind Malaysia's data centre boom are striking. Between 2021 and June 2025, Malaysian authorities approved 143 data centre investment projects with a combined value of RM144.4 billion — a figure that dwarfs comparable pipelines in neighbouring markets. Crucially, more than two-thirds of all data centre capacity currently under construction in Southeast Asia is located in Malaysia, cementing the country's lead as the region's preferred destination for large-scale digital infrastructure. Johor, in particular, has emerged as a global focal point, attracting massive campus-style facilities from some of the world's largest technology companies due to its proximity to Singapore and lower land and electricity costs.

The roster of investors reads like a who's who of global technology: Amazon Web Services, Google, Microsoft, Oracle, and TikTok's parent company ByteDance are among the major players that have committed to significant data centre footprints in Malaysia. These commitments carry substantial downstream economic benefits, including the creation of high-skilled jobs, the development of local supply chains, and increased demand for supporting services ranging from engineering and construction to cybersecurity and facility management. The government has pointed to these investments as validation of Malaysia's Malaysia Digital (MD) status programme, which offers a package of tax incentives and operational privileges to qualifying technology companies.

The government has also moved to ensure that the data centre pipeline aligns with national AI ambitions rather than simply adding raw capacity. An AI moratorium introduced in late 2025 shifted the approval process toward facilities specifically designed to support AI workloads, requiring applicants to demonstrate credible plans for AI-ready infrastructure, energy efficiency, and local talent development. The policy shift was designed to prevent Malaysia from becoming merely a host for commodity cloud storage while ensuring that the economic value of the data centre boom is maximised for the local ecosystem.

KPMG's analysis highlights that sustaining this growth trajectory will require continued attention to infrastructure challenges, particularly power supply reliability and water availability for cooling. Concerns have been raised about the strain that large-scale data centres place on the national electricity grid, and the government has been urged to accelerate investment in renewable energy sources to ensure that Malaysia's data centre expansion is both commercially competitive and environmentally sustainable. With the right policy frameworks and infrastructure investments in place, analysts believe Malaysia is well-positioned to translate its current data centre leadership into a durable strategic advantage for the digital economy era.
