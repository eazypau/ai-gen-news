Cybercrime Bill 2026 Among Key Bills Tabled in Dewan Rakyat Session

Bernama · 23 June 2026 · Politics

The Dewan Rakyat parliamentary session that commenced on June 22 and will sit until July 16 is set to be one of the most legislatively active in recent memory, with a raft of significant bills awaiting debate and passage. Among the most anticipated is the Cybercrime Bill 2026, which seeks to modernise Malaysia's legal framework for prosecuting online crimes including hacking, digital fraud, cyberstalking, and the distribution of harmful content. The bill has been in preparation for several years and is expected to draw both broad support and targeted criticism from civil liberties advocates concerned about provisions that could be misused to suppress legitimate online expression.

Alongside the Cybercrime Bill, lawmakers will also consider amendments to the Road Transport Act 1987, which is being updated to address a range of issues including stiffer penalties for dangerous driving, updated provisions for electric vehicles, and revised licensing categories for new classes of personal mobility devices that have proliferated on Malaysian roads. The amendments follow a series of high-profile road accidents and growing public pressure on the government to take a firmer stance on traffic law enforcement.

A particularly significant item on the legislative agenda is the Constitution Amendment Bill (No.2) 2026, which proposes the formal separation of the roles of the Attorney General and the Public Prosecutor. Currently, both roles are held by the same individual, a constitutional arrangement that legal reform advocates have long argued creates a structural conflict of interest and leaves the prosecutorial process vulnerable to political interference. The proposed separation has garnered widespread support from the legal fraternity, civil society organisations, and several members of parliament on both sides of the aisle.

The Gender Parity Bill is another landmark piece of proposed legislation that will be debated during the session. Introduced by a parliamentary special select committee, the bill would require political parties to field a minimum of 30 percent women candidates in both general and state elections. Malaysia currently ranks well below the global average for female parliamentary representation, and proponents argue that without a mandatory threshold, voluntary party commitments to gender balance have repeatedly fallen short. Women's rights groups and several prominent female politicians from across the political spectrum have thrown their support behind the measure.

The government is expected to prioritise the passage of these bills before the session ends on July 16, given that the Johor state election on July 11 will likely thin the attendance of MPs with constituencies in that state. Parliamentary observers have noted that the government will need to manage the legislative calendar carefully to ensure quorum is maintained for votes on the constitutional amendment bills, which require a two-thirds majority in both houses. The opposition's internal divisions may paradoxically benefit the government's legislative agenda by reducing the effectiveness of coordinated opposition tactics during the session.
