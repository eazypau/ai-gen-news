Gender Parity Bill Proposes 30% Women Candidates Requirement in Elections

The Star · 23 June 2026 · Politics

A parliamentary special select committee has tabled the Gender Parity Bill, a landmark legislative proposal that would require all political parties to field a minimum of 30 percent women candidates in both parliamentary and state elections. The bill, which is expected to be debated during the current Dewan Rakyat session running until July 16, addresses what its proponents describe as a fundamental structural barrier to women's participation in Malaysian democratic politics. If passed, it would represent one of the most significant reforms to Malaysia's electoral legislation in the country's history.

Malaysia's record on female political representation remains poor by both regional and global standards. Women currently make up fewer than 15 percent of members of parliament, a figure that places Malaysia behind several of its ASEAN neighbours and well below the global average of approximately 26 percent. Successive governments have set voluntary targets for increasing female representation, but these targets have consistently been missed as political parties — particularly in the selection of candidates for winnable seats — have continued to prioritise male candidates. Advocates for the bill argue that without a statutory mandate, meaningful progress will remain elusive.

The 30 percent threshold proposed in the bill is consistent with the standard recommended by international bodies including the United Nations and the Inter-Parliamentary Union as a minimum critical mass for women to exercise meaningful influence within legislative bodies. Under the proposed legislation, parties that fail to meet the 30 percent requirement would face penalties that could include disqualification of excess male candidates or financial sanctions. The exact enforcement mechanism is understood to be one of the areas still being refined ahead of the bill's formal second reading.

Women's rights organisations and civil society groups have been among the most vocal supporters of the bill, with groups such as the Joint Action Group for Gender Equality and All Women's Action Society (AWAM) issuing statements calling on parliamentarians to support its passage. Several prominent female politicians from both the government and opposition benches have also expressed public support, though some have noted that the bill's success will ultimately depend on parties also committing to place women candidates in competitive seats rather than unwinnable constituencies where the 30 percent threshold can be met as a statistical formality.

Resistance to the bill is anticipated from some quarters within the political establishment, where concerns have been raised about the appropriateness of government mandating party candidate selection processes and the practical challenges of finding sufficient numbers of qualified women candidates in all constituencies within a short timeframe. Supporters counter that the pipeline of qualified women in politics, civil service, business, and community leadership is deep, and that the main obstacle has always been the willingness of party gatekeepers to give women a genuine opportunity. The bill's progress through parliament is being watched closely as a test of the unity government's stated commitment to gender equality as a national priority.
