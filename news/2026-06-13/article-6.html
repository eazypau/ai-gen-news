Malaysia Has the Chip Investment — It Doesn't Have Enough Engineers to Back It Up

SEMICON Southeast Asia 2026, held in Kuala Lumpur this week, shone a spotlight on a critical structural bottleneck threatening to undermine Malaysia's semiconductor ambitions: a severe and widening talent gap at the engineering and technical specialist level. Despite Malaysia securing RM28.5 billion in electrical and electronics (E&E) sector investment approvals in 2025, industry leaders warn that the pipeline of qualified graduates is not keeping pace with demand.

Several multinational chip makers and equipment suppliers operating in Penang — Malaysia's Silicon Valley equivalent — reported vacancy rates of 15–25% in engineering roles, citing an insufficient supply of graduates with relevant degrees in microelectronics, materials science, and electrical engineering. Universities are expanding enrolment but graduates take years to develop the specialised on-the-job experience that chip fabs require.

The government has recognised the urgency of the skills challenge. In May 2026, the Ministry of Investment, Trade and Industry (MITI) announced an expanded Semiconductor Industry Talent Development Fund, while MIDA is working with HRDC to offer subsidised upskilling programmes. A fast-track work permit scheme for experienced foreign engineers was also introduced in early 2026, with Employment Pass Category III thresholds revised upward effective June 1.

Industry associations including the Penang Semiconductor Industry Association have called for even more aggressive action, including dual-degree programmes between local universities and overseas technical institutions, greater industry involvement in curriculum design, and guaranteed internship placements at chip fabrication and packaging facilities. Some firms are also exploring automation to partially offset the human resource shortage.

Analysts caution that if the talent gap is not addressed within the next three to five years, some semiconductor investors may redirect their expansion plans to competing markets such as Vietnam, Thailand, or India, which are aggressively courting the same foreign direct investment Malaysia is targeting. The talent challenge is now considered the single biggest constraint on Malaysia's semiconductor growth trajectory.
