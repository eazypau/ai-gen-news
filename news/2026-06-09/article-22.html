Should Senior Drivers Undergo Medical Exams Before Renewing Licences? Malaysia Debates

Lifestyle Sin Chew Daily 9 June 2026

A national conversation has emerged in Malaysia over whether senior citizens should be required to pass a medical fitness examination before renewing their driving licences, as the country moves rapidly toward becoming an ageing society. The debate was reignited by a recent series of road accidents involving elderly drivers, prompting road safety advocates, medical professionals, and policymakers to revisit the current system, which imposes no age-specific health requirements for licence renewal.

Proponents of mandatory medical checks for older drivers point to declining vision, slower reaction times, and a higher prevalence of conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and early-stage cognitive impairment among Malaysia's senior population. They argue that a structured medical screening programme — similar to those adopted in Japan, South Korea, and several European countries — would identify drivers whose health conditions render them unsafe on the road, potentially preventing serious accidents and saving lives.

Malaysia's road death toll remains one of the highest in Southeast Asia on a per-capita basis, and improving driver fitness standards is seen by the Road Safety Department (JKJR) as one lever among several needed to bring fatality rates down toward the government's Vision Zero targets. The department is reportedly studying a tiered approach, where drivers above a certain age — potentially 65 or 70 — would be required to present a medical certificate of fitness at each licence renewal cycle.

Opponents of the proposal argue that it would unfairly penalise older Malaysians, particularly those living in rural or peri-urban areas with limited access to public transport. For many senior citizens, a driving licence is not a luxury but a lifeline — enabling them to access medical facilities, grocery stores, and family members independently. Critics warn that a poorly designed policy could strip millions of elderly Malaysians of their mobility without adequate alternative transport provisions being in place.

Healthcare practitioners have also raised practical implementation concerns: a meaningful medical screening for driving fitness goes beyond a simple doctor's signature and should include standardised vision tests, reaction time assessments, and cognitive screening — a process that would strain Malaysia's primary healthcare system if applied at scale. The debate reflects broader tensions around how Malaysia's infrastructure and social policy can adapt to serve a rapidly ageing population.
