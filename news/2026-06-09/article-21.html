Developed Nations Continue Shipping Plastic Waste to Malaysia Despite Global Bans

Environment Sin Chew Daily 9 June 2026

Despite international agreements aimed at curbing the export of plastic waste from wealthy nations to developing countries, Malaysia continues to receive large volumes of mixed and contaminated plastic refuse from Europe and North America. Environmental monitoring groups report that the flow of waste has not significantly diminished since the Basel Convention amendments were strengthened, with some shipments arriving under mislabelled manifests that describe the contents as recyclable materials rather than waste.

Malaysia, along with Indonesia and Turkey, has repeatedly featured on lists of the world's top recipients of plastic waste exports from high-income countries. While some portion of these imports is genuinely processed by licensed recycling facilities, investigations by environmental NGOs have found that a significant share ends up in illegal dump sites, burned in open fields, or discharged into waterways — creating serious public health hazards and long-term environmental contamination.

The Malaysian government has taken steps to address the problem, including a directive to strengthen customs controls and increase inspections of incoming plastic waste shipments at Port Klang and other major entry points. The Department of Environment has increased enforcement actions against illegal recycling operations and issued fines to operators found processing prohibited waste streams.

However, enforcement advocates say the problem persists because the economic incentives remain tilted in favour of accepting cheap or free plastic waste from overseas. Legitimate Malaysian recyclers find it difficult to compete with operators who process low-quality imported waste at minimal cost, undermining the development of a sustainable domestic recycling economy built on locally generated materials.

Environmental groups are calling on Malaysia to pursue bilateral agreements with key source countries to reduce plastic waste exports at origin rather than relying solely on import controls. They argue that wealthy nations must take greater responsibility for their own waste streams, investing in domestic recycling infrastructure rather than externalising the cost and environmental harm to lower-income countries.
