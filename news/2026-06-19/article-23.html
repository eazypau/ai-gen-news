Malaysia's Flood Losses Ease But Infrastructure Damage Remains Costly, World Bank Warns of Climate Risk

Environment Eco-Business / World Bank 19 June 2026

Malaysia's annual flood-related losses climbed to RM933.4 million (approximately USD 218.3 million) in 2024 — equivalent to 0.05% of GDP — rising from RM755.4 million in 2023, according to data tracked by the Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre and Eco-Business. While total flood losses eased by 32% in 2025 compared to the prior year, infrastructure damage remained elevated, indicating that the country's built environment continues to bear the brunt of increasingly severe weather events.

Recent flooding in May 2026 brought renewed attention to the issue, with at least 47 people reported sheltering in relief camps in Kuala Muda, Kedah, and at least 315 displaced residents in Pontian, Johor, following flooding events on May 17, 2026. These incidents underscore the persistent vulnerability of communities in low-lying and coastal areas during periods of intense rainfall, which meteorologists attribute to a combination of seasonal monsoon patterns and the amplifying effects of climate change.

A joint World Bank-Bank Negara Malaysia study on climate and financial risk projected that higher and more frequent rainfall — driven by rising global temperatures — will expose Malaysia to significantly greater flood risks in the coming decades. The study estimated that without substantial investment in flood defences, early warning systems, and climate-resilient infrastructure, the economic costs of flooding could increase substantially as a share of GDP by mid-century.

Unsustainable human practices have compounded the problem. Deforestation of highland areas, the development of flood plains without adequate drainage infrastructure, and land degradation have increased surface runoff and reduced the natural buffering capacity of Malaysia's watershed systems. Urban planning experts have called for more rigorous application of environmental impact assessments and stronger enforcement of building regulations in flood-prone zones.

The government has responded with increased allocations for flood mitigation infrastructure under recent national budgets, including river rehabilitation, retention pond construction, and the installation of automated flood warning systems in high-risk areas. However, civil society groups and environmental organisations have argued that the pace and scale of investment remains insufficient relative to the growing risk, and that a more comprehensive national climate adaptation strategy — with clear targets, timelines, and accountability mechanisms — is urgently needed.
