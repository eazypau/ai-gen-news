Malaysia Healthcare Sector Projected to Expand 12% by 2030

A new industry report confirms that Malaysia's healthcare sector is on track to expand by 12% over the next four years, driven by an ageing population, rising rates of non-communicable diseases, private hospital expansion, and accelerating digital health adoption across the public and private systems.

The projection, released alongside a series of major medical conferences held in Kuala Lumpur over the July 3–5 weekend, reflects broad consensus among health economists and industry players that Malaysia's healthcare market remains substantially underpenetrated relative to its GDP per capita. Spending on healthcare as a share of GDP has historically been below OECD averages, and demographic changes — particularly the rapid growth of the over-60 population — are expected to drive demand for hospital beds, specialist consultations, and pharmaceutical products.

Health Minister Dr Dzulkefly Ahmad confirmed that 2026 represents a pivotal year for health system digitalisation, with the government completing the expansion of the Total Hospital Information System (THIS) to 16 hospitals, the cloud-based Clinic Management System to approximately 2,489 primary care settings, and the Dental Information System to 157 dental clinics. These investments are expected to improve care coordination, reduce administrative burden, and ultimately lower system costs through more efficient resource allocation.

Private hospitals are also driving significant investment in new capacity, with several major hospital groups announcing expansion plans in the Klang Valley, Johor, and East Malaysia. The influx of medical tourism, particularly from patients in Indonesia, Vietnam, and the Middle East seeking specialised care in Malaysia's internationally accredited private hospitals, is an additional growth driver that the Ministry of Health and Tourism Malaysia have been working to cultivate through joint promotion campaigns.

Despite the growth outlook, health sector analysts warn of persistent challenges including a shortage of specialist doctors and nurses, rising medical insurance premiums that are making private healthcare inaccessible for middle-income Malaysians, and the need for better integration between public and private healthcare systems to avoid costly duplication of services. Addressing these structural issues will be essential to ensuring that the sector's growth translates into better health outcomes for all Malaysians rather than just for those who can afford premium care.
