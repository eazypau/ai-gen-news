PM Anwar's Constitutional Amendment for PM Term Limit Fails to Pass

Bloomberg / New Straits Times 18 June 2026 Politics

A landmark constitutional amendment bill proposed by Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim to impose a 10-year term limit on the office of the prime minister has failed to secure the required two-thirds parliamentary majority. The bill, which would have been one of the most significant democratic reforms in Malaysia's history, fell short of votes in March 2026 when a number of lawmakers from coalition partners withheld their support.

The defeat was widely seen as a significant setback to Anwar's reform agenda, which he had championed since coming to power in late 2022. The prime minister had proposed the amendment in January 2026, framing it as a measure to prevent the concentration of power and safeguard democratic governance. He had also introduced several other reform bills at the same time, including a Separation of the Attorney-General and Prosecutor roles, an Ombudsman Act, and a Freedom of Information Act.

Coalition partners within the unity government reportedly had reservations about the constitutional amendment, with some expressing concern over the breadth of the reform and its implications for future prime ministers from their parties. The failure highlighted the difficulties of pursuing meaningful political reform within a broad coalition government comprising parties with divergent interests.

Despite the setback, the Anwar administration has pushed forward with other legislative initiatives. The Parliamentary Service Bill, aimed at separating parliamentary operations from the civil service, was introduced and is seen as a step toward greater legislative independence from the executive branch. Education reforms, including lowering the school entry age and expanding access to early education, have also progressed.

Political analysts say the failed amendment may dampen Anwar's reform credentials ahead of the next general election. Critics within civil society have called on the prime minister to reintroduce the bill with a renewed coalition-building effort, while government supporters argue that incremental reform remains preferable to political deadlock.
